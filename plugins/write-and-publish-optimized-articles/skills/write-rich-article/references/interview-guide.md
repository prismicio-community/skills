# Interview Guide (Phase 3)

There are two moments where the user gets asked something, and they do different jobs. The **Phase 0 scoping ask** is three tapped questions that stop the wrong article being researched. This, the **Phase 3 deep interview**, comes after the research and asks only what the research could not answer. Never move Phase 3 material into Phase 0: asking someone for their unique angle before showing them what the competition says produces a generic answer, and it wastes the one moment their attention is guaranteed.

The interview exists because research produces consensus and consensus produces forgettable articles. The user has judgement, scar tissue, and a stake in being right that no amount of searching can substitute for.

It only works if the questions are informed. An interview conducted before the research wastes the user's time asking things that were findable; an interview conducted after it can ask the one question the research could not answer.

## Rules

**Come with a hypothesis.** Every question should carry what was found and what is being proposed, so the user can approve, correct, or reject in a few words. The user should be able to answer the whole batch in three minutes if they are busy, or expand on any one of them if they care.

**One batch, 5–8 numbered questions.** Not a conversation with one question per turn. Not a form with twenty fields.

**Give each question a reason.** One clause is enough: "asking because the top three results all disagree with your positioning doc here."

**Offer a default.** Where a defensible answer already exists from the research, propose it: "My assumption is X. Say 'yes' and I'll run with it."

**Never ask what the research already answered.** If the interview asks who the audience is, Phase 1 was done badly.

**Stop after asking.** Wait for the reply. Do not continue into the outline in the same turn.

## What every interview must cover

### 1. The contradiction (1–3 questions)
Where public consensus and internal evidence disagree, or where the ranking pages are confidently wrong. This is the article's angle. State the contradiction, propose the position, ask if it holds.

> "Every top-10 result treats this as a tooling problem. Two of your call transcripts frame it as an ownership problem between design and engineering. Lead with the ownership framing?"

### 2. The proprietary claim (1 question)
What can this company say that nobody else can? Internal data, a repeated pattern across customers, a named framework, a build decision with a reason behind it. Ask whether numbers can be attached and whether they are publishable.

### 3. The unproven conviction (1 question)
What does the user believe that they cannot prove yet? These are often the most interesting sentences in an article, and they are safe as long as they are framed as a position rather than a fact. Ask for it explicitly, and confirm it can be labelled as a prediction or an opinion.

### 4. The honest limitation (1 question)
Where does the brand's product genuinely not fit, and how bluntly can that be said? The honesty rule in the writing standards requires naming at least one limitation. Getting the user's own version means it will be specific rather than defanged.

### 5. Constraints (1 question)
Claims to avoid, competitors not to name, legal or positioning red lines, anything the company has publicly said it will not do. Cheaper to ask now than to unpick after the draft.

### 6. Links to use (1 question, always)
Ask directly for:
- Their own pages, docs, case studies, or research to link to.
- External sources they trust and want cited.
- Anything they specifically do not want linked.
- Anchor text preferences, if they have any.

Say what will happen with them: every supplied link gets checked for a 200 response and placed only where it makes contextual sense, and anything that cannot be placed honestly will be reported rather than forced in.

### 7. Format and scope (optional, only if genuinely open)
Only ask when the research left it ambiguous: whether to cover a borderline subtopic, whether to include a competitor, whether the piece is one article or two.

## Handling the answers

- **Terse answers are fine.** "Yes, yes, no, skip" is a complete interview. Use it.
- **Partial answers**: proceed, and list the unanswered questions as open assumptions in the outline.
- **An answer that contradicts the research**: do not just take it. Say what the evidence shows, then follow the user's call if they hold. They may know something the sources do not, and it is worth recording which it is.
- **A vague answer to the POV question**: one follow-up, made concrete. "Can you point at a moment where you saw this go wrong?" A story yields a usable paragraph; an abstraction does not.

## Carrying POVs into the article

Every POV from the interview must land somewhere specific, or it evaporates during drafting. Track each one as: the POV, where it appears (section or FAQ), and what evidence supports it. If a POV has no home in the outline, either the outline is missing a section or the POV was too vague to use. Resolve it before writing, not during.
